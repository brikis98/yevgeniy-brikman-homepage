In Paris in 1895, Alfred Dreyfus, a young Jewish officer in the French army, is convicted of treason, stripped of his rank in front of a jeering crowd of twenty thousand, and sentenced to life imprisonment on Devil's Island. Watching his humiliation is Georges Picquart, the ambitious, newly promoted head of the counterespionage agency that "proved" Dreyfus passed secrets to the Germans, and who is convinced of his guilt. But not long after Dreyfus is shipped off to prison, Picquart stumbles on information suggesting that there is still a spy at large in the French military. I had heard the term "The Dreyfus Affair" before, but never knew the details, and this book provides a fascinating account of what happened, in the form of a fun historical spy thriller story.

<!--more-->

The details of the Dreyfus affair are crazy. It's a tale of anti-semitism, incompetence, and political cover up, and one of those real-life stories where the bad guys just keep doubling down over and over again. In this case, justice eventually prevails; if only that were the case with the current US administration...

The characters in the story, almost all of them based on real people, felt real. Georges Picquart makes for an especially interesting protagonist. He works for the Statistical Section, which is the name of the French military intelligence, so the story gives you an interesting glimpse into spying and intelligence gathering of that era. 

As with many of Robert Harris' books, it touches on many other interesting aspects of that period of history: France reeling after being soundly defeated by Germany in the Franco-Prussian war (an interesting foreshadowing of WWI), the use of forensic handwriting analysis, Émile Zola "J'Accuse!" article, and, more generally, the role of public intellectuals (in the era of writing, intellectuals were at the top of society; in the era of TV and Internet, illiterate morons run the world).

Overall, another fantastic book from Robert Harris.
