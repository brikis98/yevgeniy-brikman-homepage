It is June 1941, the war is going badly for England, and across the North Sea, 18-year-old Harald Olufsen takes a shortcut on the German-occupied Danish island of Sande and stumbles onto a discovery that could change the course of the war. He has to get word to England, but he has no way of getting there. All he has is an old, derelict Hornet Moth biplane rusting away in a ruined church, a plane so decrepit it may never get off the ground, even if Harald knew how to fly it. It's a fascinating backdrop for a book, and apparently inspired by real events, though as far as spy thrillers go, the execution is only so-so.

<!--more-->

The biggest miss in the book is that it didn't actually feel all that thrilling. It's a competently-told story, but given the stakes involved, it never really had me on the edge of my seat. There are plenty of WWII historical fiction books like _[The Nightingale](/blog/2026/02/16/the-nightingale/)_ and _[Munich](/blog/2026/06/29/munich/)_, and more general thrillers like _[The Da Vinci Code](/blog/2002/01/01/the-da-vinci-code/)_, that do a much better job of grabbing you, and are impossible to put down. This is not one of them.

I think part of the issue is that the main character is a young boy who is just finishing school, and it gives the book a distinctly young adult feel. Most of the other characters we meet are adults, and are more fully fleshed out, whereas Harald always remains as a young, 2-dimensional, unreasonably-brave protagonist going through a coming-of-age story.

That said, the book is still an intriguing look at the war, with glimpses into what happened to Denmark, the role of women, the importance of technologies like radar, and so on. With a better protagonist, and a slightly tweaked plot structure, this would've been 5 stars, but as it is, it's a good, but not great, 4-star read.
